# The Salmoscan story

This page is about **why** and **how** Salmoscan came to be — market need, technical challenges, partnerships, and R&D. **Measured results, key figures, and operational value** are all on the homepage under [Product](/#product).

## Background

Salmoscan was born from a pressing need for better knowledge and accurate analysis of farmed fish on wellboats. Current solutions are widely seen as not accurate enough. Most machine vision systems focus on weight and fish count, with accuracy often quoted at a few percent under “optimal” conditions. The catch is that those optimal conditions rarely match what happens in real operations.

When we started the Salmoscan project, we looked at where it makes most sense to analyse fish, which parameters matter to the industry, and what is technically feasible. We quickly found the challenges were far from simple: fish either behaved like “herring in a barrel” in water-filled pipes, or were briefly in air at high speed in a chaotic spray environment.

## How we built it

Mohn Technology has long, broad experience in computer vision, custom lighting, and AI. For this project we set an ambitious goal: capture images good enough to see the earliest sea lice stages on fish passing the camera at up to 5 m/s. That requires not only high camera resolution, but a highly capable lighting system and substantial compute to analyse the images.

We began in our workshop with a lab setup to test different cameras, lenses, and lighting approaches. At the same time we reached out to researchers and industry contacts to explore collaboration and data collection.

Robust, accurate AI algorithms need large amounts of precise data. We were soon on fish farms with biologists who manually analysed fish to build datasets. Data were sorted, annotated, and used to train AI models on our high-performance server.

In parallel we ran user research on which data the industry cares about, built a web portal for the user interface, and produced camera housing prototypes.

In R&D it is important to test and rule in or out as many candidate solutions as early as possible — so you see quickly which paths are worth following and which are dead ends. We found, for example, that some approaches to estimating fish weight showed very strong accuracy potential. We also began developing models for sea lice counting, wounds, and scale loss.
